AB Factory

A/B decisions are a solved problem — until they aren't. A deterministic rule gets 100% of verdicts right on 100 cases built from five textbook mechanisms. On 660 cases built from 33, it gets 42.6% — and 20 of those mechanisms it never catches once. The corpus that breaks it — and the tools to see exactly where.

This is not a straw man. The rule checks exactly what an analyst checks: is the effect significant, does it clear the practical threshold, did any guardrail drop, do the segments disagree. In that order. That is how decision checklists are built — and there is nothing naive about it.

Nothing in the table says no. Significant, far above the threshold, guardrail fine. The rule shipped it — and so would most checklists.

What the table does not show: about 14% of test-group sessions came from datacenter IPs with bot-like burst patterns, and there is nothing similar in control. The lift is measured on traffic that is partly not human.

The feature is not judged here — the measurement is. The correct answer is not «do not ship» but «investigate»: filter the traffic and measure again.

This clue lives in the case description, not in the results table. The rule only reads the table — which is exactly why it misses 20 of the 33 mechanisms.

Why the rule misses

The design is simple, and that is the point. The rule compares numbers against thresholds: significance against alpha, effect against the practical threshold, guardrail against the allowed drop. That catches a trap when the trap reduces to «one number crossed a line». Half the mechanisms in the corpus are not built that way: to see peeking, Simpson’s paradox or bot traffic you have to look at the circumstances of the experiment, not at the results table. There are no thresholds for that.
